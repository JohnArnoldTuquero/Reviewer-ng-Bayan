What is the Cartesian Coordinate System?

The Cartesian Coordinate System is a system used to locate points on a plane using pairs of numbers called coordinates.

It is named after the French mathematician and philosopher René Descartes.

The system uses two perpendicular number lines:

x-axis — the horizontal axis.

y-axis — the vertical axis.

Every point on the coordinate plane can be identified using an ordered pair written as:

(x, y)

The first number tells us the horizontal position, while the second number tells us the vertical position.

The Coordinate Plane

The coordinate plane is the two-dimensional surface formed by the intersection of the x-axis and y-axis.

The horizontal x-axis and vertical y-axis divide the plane into four regions called quadrants.

Numbers increase as we move to the right on the x-axis and as we move upward on the y-axis.

Numbers decrease as we move to the left on the x-axis and as we move downward on the y-axis.

A coordinate plane allows us to graph points, lines, functions, geometric shapes, and other mathematical relationships.

The x-axis and y-axis

x-axis

The x-axis is the horizontal number line.

Points on the x-axis have a y-coordinate of zero.

For example:

(4, 0)

(-3, 0)

y-axis

The y-axis is the vertical number line.

Points on the y-axis have an x-coordinate of zero.

For example:

(0, 5)

(0, -2)

The x-axis and y-axis intersect at the origin.

The Origin

The point where the x-axis and y-axis intersect is called the origin.

The coordinates of the origin are:

(0, 0)

The origin is the reference point for the entire coordinate plane.

From the origin:

Moving right increases the x-coordinate.

Moving left decreases the x-coordinate.

Moving up increases the y-coordinate.

Moving down decreases the y-coordinate.

For example, starting at (0, 0), moving 3 units right and 2 units up gives the point:

(3, 2)

The Four Quadrants

The x-axis and y-axis divide the coordinate plane into four quadrants.

Quadrant I

Both coordinates are positive:

(+, +)

Example: (3, 4)

Quadrant II

The x-coordinate is negative and the y-coordinate is positive:

(-, +)

Example: (-3, 4)

Quadrant III

Both coordinates are negative:

(-, -)

Example: (-3, -4)

Quadrant IV

The x-coordinate is positive and the y-coordinate is negative:

(+, -)

Example: (3, -4)

The quadrants are numbered counterclockwise, beginning with Quadrant I in the upper-right region.

Ordered Pairs

An ordered pair consists of two numbers written in parentheses:

(x, y)

The first coordinate is always the x-coordinate.

The second coordinate is always the y-coordinate.

For example:

(5, 2)

means:

Move 5 units horizontally from the origin.

Then move 2 units vertically.

The order is important.

(5, 2) is different from (2, 5).

Plotting Points

To plot a point on the coordinate plane, follow the ordered pair from left to right.

For example, plot:

(4, 3)

Step 1: Start at the origin (0, 0).

Step 2: Move 4 units to the right because the x-coordinate is 4.

Step 3: Move 3 units upward because the y-coordinate is 3.

Step 4: Mark the point.

The point is located in Quadrant I.

Example with Negative Coordinates

Plot:

(-3, 2)

Move 3 units left and then 2 units up.

The point is located in Quadrant II.

Reading Coordinates

To read the coordinates of a point, determine its horizontal and vertical positions relative to the origin.

Always read the x-coordinate first and the y-coordinate second.

For example, if a point is 6 units to the right and 4 units below the origin, its coordinates are:

(6, -4)

If a point is 5 units to the left and 3 units above the origin:

(-5, 3)

Carefully check the direction of each coordinate before writing the ordered pair.

Positive and Negative Coordinates

The sign of a coordinate tells you the direction in which you move from the origin.

Positive x → move right.

Negative x → move left.

Positive y → move up.

Negative y → move down.

For example:

(4, 3) means right 4 and up 3.

(-4, 3) means left 4 and up 3.

(-4, -3) means left 4 and down 3.

(4, -3) means right 4 and down 3.

Distance Between Points

The distance formula can be used to find the distance between two points on the coordinate plane.

For points:

(x1, y1) and (x2, y2)

the distance formula is:

d = √[(x2 - x1)2 + (y2 - y1)2]

Example

Find the distance between (1, 2) and (4, 6).

Substitute the values:

d = √[(4 - 1)2 + (6 - 2)2]

d = √[32 + 42]

d = √[9 + 16]

d = √25

Therefore:

d = 5

Midpoint of a Line Segment

The midpoint is the point exactly halfway between two endpoints.

The midpoint formula is:

M = ((x1 + x2)/2, (y1 + y2)/2)

Example

Find the midpoint of (2, 4) and (6, 8).

Substitute the coordinates:

M = ((2 + 6)/2, (4 + 8)/2)

Simplify:

M = (8/2, 12/2)

Therefore:

M = (4, 6)

The midpoint divides the line segment into two equal parts.

Slope of a Line

The slope measures the steepness and direction of a line.

The slope formula is:

m = (y2 - y1) / (x2 - x1)

It can also be described as:

Rise / Run

Example

Find the slope of the line passing through (1, 2) and (4, 8).

m = (8 - 2)/(4 - 1)

m = 6/3

Therefore:

m = 2

Types of Slope

Positive slope: The line rises from left to right.

Negative slope: The line falls from left to right.

Zero slope: The line is horizontal.

Undefined slope: The line is vertical.

x-intercept and y-intercept

An intercept is a point where a graph crosses one of the coordinate axes.

x-intercept

The x-intercept is the point where the graph crosses the x-axis.

At the x-axis:

y = 0

y-intercept

The y-intercept is the point where the graph crosses the y-axis.

At the y-axis:

x = 0

Example

Consider:

y = 2x + 4

To find the y-intercept, set x = 0:

y = 2(0) + 4 = 4

The y-intercept is:

(0, 4)

To find the x-intercept, set y = 0:

0 = 2x + 4

-4 = 2x

x = -2

The x-intercept is:

(-2, 0)

Symmetry on the Coordinate Plane

A graph has symmetry when one part of the graph is a reflection of another part.

Symmetry Across the x-axis

Reflecting a point across the x-axis changes the sign of the y-coordinate:

(x, y) → (x, -y)

Example:

(3, 5) → (3, -5)

Symmetry Across the y-axis

Reflecting a point across the y-axis changes the sign of the x-coordinate:

(x, y) → (-x, y)

Example:

(3, 5) → (-3, 5)

Symmetry Across the Origin

Reflecting a point across the origin changes the signs of both coordinates:

(x, y) → (-x, -y)

Example:

(3, 5) → (-3, -5)

Applications of the Coordinate System

The Cartesian coordinate system is used in many areas of mathematics and everyday applications.

Geometry

Coordinates can be used to locate points, calculate distances, find midpoints, and study geometric shapes.

Graphs and Functions

Functions can be represented visually by plotting ordered pairs on the coordinate plane.

Navigation

Coordinate systems can be used to describe positions and locations.

Maps

Coordinate systems provide a mathematical way to identify positions on a map or grid.

Computer Graphics

Computer programs use coordinate systems to position objects, shapes, images, and other elements on a screen.

Common Mistakes

1. Reversing the coordinates

Always remember:

(x, y)

The x-coordinate comes first and the y-coordinate comes second.

2. Moving in the wrong direction

Positive x means right, negative x means left.

Positive y means up, negative y means down.

3. Confusing the axes

The x-axis is horizontal and the y-axis is vertical.

4. Misidentifying the quadrant

Check the signs of both coordinates before determining the quadrant.

5. Forgetting zero coordinates

A point with a zero coordinate lies directly on an axis, not inside a quadrant.

For example:

(4, 0) lies on the x-axis.

(0, 4) lies on the y-axis.

6. Using the wrong formula

When solving coordinate geometry problems, carefully identify whether the problem requires distance, midpoint, slope, or intercepts.

Summary

The Cartesian Coordinate System is used to locate points on a plane using ordered pairs.

The coordinate plane consists of two perpendicular axes: the x-axis and y-axis.

The x-axis is horizontal, while the y-axis is vertical.

Their intersection is called the origin, with coordinates (0, 0).

A point is represented by an ordered pair:

(x, y)

The coordinate plane is divided into four quadrants:

Quadrant I: (+, +)

Quadrant II: (-, +)

Quadrant III: (-, -)

Quadrant IV: (+, -)

The distance formula is:

d = √[(x2 - x1)2 + (y2 - y1)2]

The midpoint formula is:

M = ((x1 + x2)/2, (y1 + y2)/2)

The slope formula is:

m = (y2 - y1) / (x2 - x1)

Understanding the coordinate system is essential for studying graphs, functions, geometry, equations, and analytic geometry.
