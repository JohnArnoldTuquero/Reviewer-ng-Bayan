What is Geometry?

Geometry is a branch of mathematics that studies points, lines, angles, shapes, surfaces, and solids. It examines their properties, measurements, positions, and relationships.

The word geometry comes from Greek words commonly translated as "earth measurement." Today, geometry is used in mathematics, architecture, engineering, construction, computer graphics, navigation, and many other fields.

Examples of geometric objects include:

  • A point representing a location.
  • A line representing a straight path extending indefinitely.
  • An angle formed by two rays with a common endpoint.
  • A triangle formed by three line segments.
  • A circle consisting of points in a plane that are equally distant from a fixed center.

To study geometry systematically, mathematicians use basic concepts, definitions, postulates, and theorems. These form the foundation for explaining and proving geometric relationships.

Basic Geometric Objects

Geometry begins with several basic objects that are used to describe more complicated figures.

Point

A point represents an exact location. It has no length, width, or thickness.

Line

A line is a straight path that extends infinitely in both directions.

Plane

A plane is a flat surface that extends infinitely in all directions within that surface.

Line Segment

A line segment is part of a line that has two endpoints.

Ray

A ray is part of a line that begins at one endpoint and extends infinitely in one direction.

Angle

An angle is formed by two rays that share a common endpoint.

Points, lines, and planes are especially important because they serve as the foundation for defining many other geometric concepts.

Undefined Terms

Undefined terms are basic geometric concepts that are accepted without formal definitions. They are used to explain and define other terms in geometry.

In elementary Euclidean geometry, the three fundamental undefined terms are:

  • Point
  • Line
  • Plane

These concepts can be described informally, but their descriptions rely on ideas that are already understood rather than on simpler formal definitions.

For example, we can describe a point as a location, but a formal definition using other geometric terms would require us to explain those terms first. Instead, geometry accepts the idea of a point as a basic starting concept.

Undefined terms are not meaningless or unimportant. They provide the foundation on which geometric definitions and logical arguments are built.

Point

A point represents an exact location in space. It has no dimensions, meaning it has no length, width, or height.

Points are usually represented by small dots and labeled with capital letters, such as A, B, C, or P.

For example, point A may be written as:

Point A

Two points can determine a unique line. For example, distinct points A and B determine line AB.

Remember that the dot used to represent a point is only a visual symbol. The mathematical point itself has no size.

Line

A line is a straight, one-dimensional figure that extends infinitely in both directions.

A line has length but no width. It does not have endpoints.

A line passing through points A and B may be represented as:

Line AB, or \(\overleftrightarrow{AB}\).

Important properties of a line include:

  • A line extends infinitely in both directions.
  • Two distinct points determine exactly one line in Euclidean geometry.
  • A line can contain infinitely many points.
  • Two distinct lines in the same plane may intersect or be parallel.

In drawings, lines are usually shown with arrowheads at both ends to indicate that they continue indefinitely.

Plane

A plane is a flat, two-dimensional surface that extends infinitely in every direction within the surface.

A plane has length and width but no thickness.

A plane is often represented by a slanted four-sided figure in a diagram. The drawing is only a limited representation because the actual mathematical plane extends infinitely.

A plane may be named using a single capital letter, such as Plane P, or by using three noncollinear points, such as Plane ABC.

Three noncollinear points determine exactly one plane in Euclidean geometry.

Points that lie on the same plane are called coplanar points.

Defined Terms

Defined terms are geometric concepts explained using undefined terms and previously established definitions.

Unlike undefined terms, defined terms have formal explanations that describe their properties or relationships.

Examples of defined terms include:

  • Line segment
  • Ray
  • Angle
  • Parallel lines
  • Perpendicular lines
  • Polygon
  • Triangle
  • Circle

For example, a line segment can be defined as the part of a line consisting of two endpoints and all points between them.

Defined terms allow mathematicians to communicate precisely. Once a term has been defined, it can be used consistently in explanations, calculations, and proofs.

Line Segment

A line segment is a part of a line bounded by two endpoints, including the endpoints themselves.

If a segment has endpoints A and B, it is written as:

\(\overline{AB}\)

Unlike a line, a segment has a finite length.

For example, if point A is at one end of a ruler and point B is at the other end, the segment between them represents a measurable distance.

If the distance between A and B is 8 centimeters, then:

AB = 8 cm

When writing AB without the segment symbol, it commonly represents the length of the segment. The notation \(\overline{AB}\) represents the segment itself.

Ray

A ray is a part of a line that begins at one endpoint and continues infinitely in one direction.

A ray that begins at point A and passes through point B is written as:

\(\overrightarrow{AB}\)

Point A is the endpoint, and the ray extends from A through B and beyond B.

The order of the letters matters. Ray AB and ray BA generally represent different rays because they begin at different endpoints and extend in different directions.

Rays are important in geometry because two rays with a common endpoint can form an angle.

Angle

An angle is a figure formed by two rays that share a common endpoint. The common endpoint is called the vertex, and the rays are called the sides of the angle.

For example, angle ABC is written as:

\(\angle ABC\)

In this notation, B is the vertex because it is the middle letter.

Angles can be classified according to their measures:

  • Acute angle: greater than 0° but less than 90°.
  • Right angle: exactly 90°.
  • Obtuse angle: greater than 90° but less than 180°.
  • Straight angle: exactly 180°.
  • Reflex angle: greater than 180° but less than 360°.

Understanding angles helps when studying triangles, parallel lines, polygons, and other geometric figures.

Mathematical Definitions

A definition is a precise statement that explains the meaning of a mathematical term.

In geometry, definitions are developed from undefined terms and concepts that have already been defined.

A good mathematical definition must be clear and precise. It should describe exactly what makes an object belong to a particular category.

Example 1: Parallel Lines

Parallel lines are coplanar lines that do not intersect, no matter how far they are extended.

For example, two distinct horizontal lines in the same plane that never meet are parallel.

Example 2: Perpendicular Lines

Perpendicular lines are lines that intersect to form right angles.

Each angle formed by two intersecting perpendicular lines measures 90°.

Example 3: Midpoint

The midpoint of a line segment is the point that divides the segment into two congruent segments.

If M is the midpoint of segment AB, then:

AM = MB

Example 4: Triangle

A triangle is a polygon with three sides, three vertices, and three interior angles.

Definitions are important because geometric proofs rely on the exact meaning of mathematical terms.

Postulates

A postulate is a statement accepted as true without proof within a particular mathematical system.

Postulates serve as starting points for reasoning. They are used together with definitions and previously established results to prove theorems.

In Euclidean geometry, examples of basic postulates include the following:

Postulate 1: A Unique Line Through Two Points

Through any two distinct points, exactly one line can be drawn.

For example, two distinct points A and B determine a unique line AB.

Postulate 2: A Line Contains at Least Two Points

Every line contains at least two distinct points. In Euclidean geometry, a line actually contains infinitely many points.

Postulate 3: A Plane Contains at Least Three Noncollinear Points

Every plane contains at least three points that do not all lie on the same line.

Postulate 4: A Unique Plane Through Three Noncollinear Points

Through any three noncollinear points, exactly one plane can be drawn.

These examples express foundational properties of Euclidean geometry. Different textbooks may state or organize their postulates differently.

Theorems

A theorem is a mathematical statement that has been established as true through logical reasoning and proof.

Unlike a postulate, a theorem requires a justification based on definitions, postulates, and previously proven theorems.

Examples of geometric theorems include:

Theorem 1: Triangle Angle Sum Theorem

The sum of the interior angles of a triangle in Euclidean geometry is 180°.

For a triangle with angles A, B, and C:

m∠A + m∠B + m∠C = 180°

Example:

If two angles measure 50° and 60°, the third angle is:

x + 50° + 60° = 180°

x + 110° = 180°

x = 70°

Theorem 2: Vertical Angles Theorem

When two lines intersect, opposite angles are congruent.

If one angle measures 75°, its vertical angle also measures 75°.

Theorem 3: Isosceles Triangle Theorem

If two sides of a triangle are congruent, then the angles opposite those sides are congruent.

Theorem 4: Pythagorean Theorem

In a right triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.

a² + b² = c²

Here, a and b represent the legs of the right triangle, while c represents the hypotenuse.

Axioms and Postulates

Axioms and postulates are both statements accepted without proof within a mathematical system.

In many school mathematics textbooks, the terms are used in similar ways. Some presentations use "axiom" for a general mathematical assumption and "postulate" for an assumption specific to geometry, but the distinction depends on the textbook and mathematical context.

For example:

  • Axiom example: If equals are added to equals, the sums are equal.
  • Geometric postulate example: Through any two distinct points, exactly one line can be drawn.

Both statements can be used as starting points for mathematical reasoning without being proven from earlier statements within the same system.

Mathematical Proofs

A proof is a logical sequence of statements that demonstrates why a mathematical claim must be true.

Geometric proofs commonly use:

  • Definitions
  • Postulates
  • Axioms
  • Previously proven theorems
  • Given information

A proof explains why a conclusion follows logically from the available information.

Example: Proving Two Angles Are Equal

Suppose two lines intersect, forming four angles. One angle and its opposite angle are vertical angles.

According to the Vertical Angles Theorem, vertical angles are congruent.

Therefore, if one of the opposite angles measures 110°, the other also measures 110°.

The conclusion is not based only on how the drawing looks. It follows from a proven geometric relationship.

Two-Column Proofs

A two-column proof organizes reasoning into two parts:

  • Statements: the facts or conclusions being established.
  • Reasons: the definitions, postulates, or theorems that justify each statement.

This format helps ensure that every step of a proof has a logical justification.

Examples and Applications

Example 1: Identifying an Undefined Term

Which of the following is an undefined term in basic Euclidean geometry: triangle, ray, or plane?

Answer: Plane.

A plane is one of the three fundamental undefined terms. A triangle and a ray are defined using more basic geometric concepts.

Example 2: Identifying a Postulate

"Through any two distinct points, exactly one line can be drawn." Is this a definition, postulate, or theorem?

Answer: Postulate.

It is accepted as a starting statement without proof in the geometric system.

Example 3: Identifying a Theorem

"The sum of the interior angles of a triangle is 180°." Is this a definition, postulate, or theorem in Euclidean geometry?

Answer: Theorem.

It can be established through a logical proof using geometric facts and postulates.

Example 4: Finding an Angle

Two angles of a triangle measure 35° and 85°. Find the third angle.

Using the Triangle Angle Sum Theorem:

35° + 85° + x = 180°

120° + x = 180°

x = 60°

The third angle measures 60°.

Example 5: Applying the Midpoint Definition

Point M is the midpoint of segment AB. If AM = 9 cm, find AB.

Since M is the midpoint:

AM = MB = 9 cm

The entire segment is:

AB = AM + MB

AB = 9 + 9 = 18 cm

Therefore, AB measures 18 cm.

Common Mistakes

When studying the foundations of geometry, avoid these common mistakes:

  • Assuming undefined terms have no meaning simply because they lack formal definitions.
  • Confusing a line with a line segment or a ray.
  • Forgetting that a line extends infinitely in both directions.
  • Assuming a postulate must be proven before it can be used.
  • Confusing a theorem with a postulate.
  • Thinking a theorem is true merely because a diagram appears to show it.
  • Using a definition without checking whether all its conditions are satisfied.
  • Writing a proof without providing reasons for its statements.
  • Confusing the geometric object with the symbol used to represent it.

Remember that diagrams help illustrate geometric ideas, but mathematical conclusions should be justified by definitions, postulates, and theorems.

Summary

Geometry studies points, lines, angles, shapes, and their relationships. Its foundations rely on undefined terms, definitions, postulates, and theorems.

  • Undefined Terms: Basic concepts accepted without formal definitions, such as point, line, and plane.
  • Definitions: Precise explanations of mathematical terms using basic concepts and previously defined terms.
  • Postulates: Statements accepted without proof within a mathematical system.
  • Axioms: Foundational statements accepted without proof, often used similarly to postulates.
  • Theorems: Statements established through logical proof.
  • Proofs: Logical arguments that explain why mathematical statements follow from accepted facts and previously established results.

The three fundamental undefined terms in basic Euclidean geometry are:

Point, Line, and Plane.

The key distinction to remember is:

Definitions explain terms, postulates provide starting assumptions, and theorems are proven using logical reasoning.
