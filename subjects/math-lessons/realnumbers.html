Real Numbers

Real numbers are all the numbers that can be represented as points on the real number line. They include numbers that can be written as fractions, decimals, whole numbers, integers, and numbers that cannot be expressed as exact fractions.

The set of real numbers is represented by the capital letter R.

Real numbers include both rational numbers and irrational numbers.


The Two Main Groups of Real Numbers

Rational Numbers: Numbers that can be written in the form a/b, where a and b are integers and b ≠ 0. Examples include 1/2, 3, -4, 0.75, and 2.5.

Irrational Numbers: Numbers that cannot be written as a fraction of two integers. Their decimal representations are non-terminating and non-repeating. Examples include √2, √3, and π.


Examples of Real Numbers

Natural Numbers: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, ...

Whole Numbers: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, ...

Integers: ..., -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, ...

Rational Numbers: 1/2, -3/4, 5, 0.25, 2.75, ...

Irrational Numbers: √2, √5, π, e, ...


Real Numbers on the Number Line

Every real number corresponds to a specific point on the real number line. Positive numbers are located to the right of zero, while negative numbers are located to the left of zero.

For example, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, and 3 are all real numbers and can be placed directly on the number line.


Properties of Real Numbers

Closure Property: Adding, subtracting, or multiplying two real numbers always produces another real number. Dividing two real numbers also produces a real number as long as the divisor is not zero.

Commutative Property: The order of numbers does not affect addition or multiplication. For example, 3 + 5 = 5 + 3 and 2 × 4 = 4 × 2.

Associative Property: The grouping of numbers does not affect addition or multiplication. For example, (2 + 3) + 4 = 2 + (3 + 4).

Distributive Property: Multiplication can be distributed over addition or subtraction. For example, 3(2 + 4) = (3 × 2) + (3 × 4).


What Is Not a Real Number?

Numbers involving the square root of a negative number are not real numbers. For example, √-1 cannot be represented as a point on the real number line. These numbers belong to the group called imaginary numbers.


Imaginary Numbers

Imaginary numbers are numbers that involve the square root of a negative number. They were introduced to allow mathematicians to solve equations that have no real-number solutions.

The basic imaginary unit is represented by the lowercase letter i.

The imaginary unit is defined as:

i = √-1


The Imaginary Unit

Since the square of any real number is positive or zero, there is no real number whose square is -1. To work with √-1, mathematicians define a new number called the imaginary unit, i.

Therefore:

i² = -1

This is the most important property of the imaginary unit.


Examples of Imaginary Numbers

√-1 = i

√-4 = 2i

√-9 = 3i

√-16 = 4i

In general, the square root of a negative number can be rewritten using the imaginary unit.

For example:

√-25 = √(25 × -1)

= √25 × √-1

= 5i


Imaginary Numbers in the Form bi

An imaginary number can commonly be written in the form bi, where b is a real number and i = √-1.

Examples include:

3i

-5i

1/2 i

√7i

The number multiplied by i is called the coefficient of the imaginary number.


Imaginary Numbers and Equations

Imaginary numbers are especially useful when solving equations that have negative values under a square root.

For example, consider the equation:

x² + 9 = 0

First, subtract 9 from both sides:

x² = -9

Take the square root of both sides:

x = √-9

Using the imaginary unit:

x = 3i

Therefore, the solution is an imaginary number.


Imaginary Numbers vs. Real Numbers

Real Numbers: Can be represented as points on the real number line. Examples include -5, 0, 2, 1/2, √2, and π.

Imaginary Numbers: Contain the imaginary unit i, where i² = -1. Examples include 2i, -4i, and 7i.


Important Note

Imaginary numbers are not "fake" numbers. The term imaginary is a historical name used for numbers involving the square root of negative numbers. They are mathematically useful and are widely used in algebra, engineering, physics, electronics, and other areas of mathematics.
